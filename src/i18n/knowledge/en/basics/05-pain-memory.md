# 🧷 Pain memory

Many people would rather sit out an attack than take something. It feels brave, but it can backfire. The nervous system learns from pain, and unfortunately it learns well.

## What it means

"Pain memory" is an image, not a medical term. Experts speak of sensitisation: nerve cells that pass on pain often and for a long time get more and more practised at it. Their threshold drops, they fire at smaller stimuli and stop later.

In migraine, this happens in two stages:

1. **During an attack.** At first only the nerves at the meninges are irritated, the throbbing. If the pain continues, nerve cells in the brainstem and thalamus switch to alarm too. Then suddenly touch hurts: combing your hair, glasses, a pillow, the shower.
2. **Over months and years.** Many severe, long attacks make the brain more irritable overall. Attacks come more easily, more often and respond less well to medication. In the worst case, migraine becomes chronic.

## Treating early pays off

![Pain rises during an attack. Before the point where touch starts to hurt, a triptan usually works well, afterwards less so](pain-memory)

A well-known study measured exactly this: people who took their triptan before touch started to hurt became completely pain-free much more often than those who took it afterwards. Taken later, it still eased the throbbing, but no longer the whole pain.

A large US study also showed: people whose acute treatment worked poorly developed chronic migraine about two and a half times as often within a year. The direction isn't certain, but the experts' message is clear: treat attacks well, don't sit them out.

## But not too much

There is a dilemma here: too many tablets cause headaches themselves, see "Headache from too much medication". The way in between:

- **Take it early and at the right dose,** rather than late and hesitantly
- **Keep an eye on the days:** triptans on fewer than 10, simple pain relievers on fewer than 15 days a month. Headly counts along
- **If your acute treatment often works poorly, tell your doctor.** Another triptan, another form or a combination can change a lot
- **If there are many attacks, discuss prevention,** see "Prevention"

## The good news

Pain memory can forget again. Sensitisation is not permanent. When attacks become rarer, for example through prevention or a medication break, the nervous system often calms down again. Even chronic migraine can become less frequent again.
