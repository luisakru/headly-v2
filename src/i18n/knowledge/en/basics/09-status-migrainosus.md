# ⏳ Status migrainosus

If an attack lasts longer than 72 hours, it is called status migrainosus. Short breaks from sleep or medication still count if the pain comes back afterwards. It is no reason to panic, but it is a reason to get help.

## Why does it happen?

Often several things come together:

- **Too many painkillers.** Taking painkillers or triptans on many days makes the brain more sensitive over time. Attacks then last longer and respond less to medication.
- **Treated too late or too little.** A medication works best early in the attack. If you wait long, it often can't catch the pain anymore.
- **Vomiting.** Tablets don't reach the blood, and you lack fluid and food. Both keep the attack going.
- **Triggers that don't stop.** Lack of sleep, stress, hormone changes around your period or caffeine withdrawal.
- **Emotional strain.** Anxiety and depression make long attacks more likely, and long attacks cause anxiety.

Sometimes there is no reason to be found. That is not your fault.

## What happens in the body?

During an attack, pain nerves around the lining of the brain are activated. If this lasts long, nerve cells in the brainstem and deeper brain switch to alarm too. Experts call this central sensitization: the pain system keeps firing even when the original stimulus fades.

One sign is that touch hurts, e.g. combing your hair, wearing glasses or lying on a pillow. From this point on, tablets often work much less well.

On top of that comes a vicious circle: pain steals sleep, nausea stops you drinking and eating, and each of these keeps the pain awake.

## What you can do at home

- Don't keep taking more of the same painkiller, stay within the daily limits. A triptan that hasn't helped twice in this attack usually won't help a third time
- A medication against nausea such as metoclopramide or domperidone helps. It also helps painkillers get absorbed better. Ask your doctor whether it suits you
- Drink enough, in small sips if you feel sick. Small, simple meals if you can
- Dark, quiet, cool. Sleep ends some attacks
- If you usually drink coffee, don't stop suddenly

At the latest when there is no end in sight after three days, call your GP or neurologist. Outside office hours, you can reach the out-of-hours medical service (116 117 in Germany), who can also come to your home; see “116 117: Help that comes to you”.

## What doctors can do

A practice or hospital has treatments you can't do at home:

- **Infusions** with painkillers such as aspirin or metamizole, plus a medication against nausea and fluids
- **Cortisone** for a few days. It can break the cycle and lowers the risk that the pain comes straight back
- **Nerve block** in some clinics: an injection with local anaesthetic at the back of the head
- **A few days in hospital** if none of this is enough, especially with painkiller overuse

Opioids such as tramadol are not a good choice for migraine. They help little and make the head more sensitive over time.

Go to the emergency room right away if you can't keep anything down or new warning signs appear, see "When to see a doctor".

## Afterwards

Status migrainosus is a good reason to take a closer look with your doctor:

- Do you take painkillers or triptans on more than 10 days a month? Then a break with medical support can help
- Would prevention make sense? See “Prevention”
- An emergency plan: what do I take when, and when do I call whom? Written down, it helps when you can't think clearly yourself

In Stats, Headly shows your medication days per month. You can bring them to your appointment.
